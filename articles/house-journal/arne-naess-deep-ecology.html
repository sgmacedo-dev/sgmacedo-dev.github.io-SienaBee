Arne Næss and the Forgotten Art of Belonging to Nature

On deep ecology, the meaning of community, and the quiet recognition that we are part of the living world.

By Silvana Macedo, Editor-in-Chief

26 October 2026

There is a particular kind of forgetfulness that accompanies civilisation. It does not arise from a lack of knowledge, but from an excess of distance. We learn to measure the natural world, to organise its resources and to alter its landscapes, yet gradually lose the simplest understanding of our place within it.

A river becomes a source of energy. A forest becomes timber. A mountain becomes a destination to be conquered or photographed. The living world is translated into a language of usefulness, and what cannot be measured in practical terms risks being overlooked.

Yet human beings are not visitors upon the Earth. We are participants in a community of life that existed long before us and will continue beyond the limits of our individual experience.

The Norwegian philosopher Arne Næss invites us to recover this forgotten sense of belonging. His philosophy of deep ecology asks us to reconsider not merely how we treat nature, but how we understand ourselves in relation to it.

Perhaps the most important environmental question is not what we ought to do with the world, but what kind of world we believe ourselves to inhabit.

The Philosopher of the Living World

Arne Næss (1912–2009), a Norwegian philosopher and mountaineer, became one of the influential figures in modern ecological thought. His work challenged the assumption that environmental problems could be resolved through technical adjustments alone.

Cleaner technologies, more efficient systems and better regulations may all have their place. But Næss recognised a deeper difficulty: the values that shape a society also shape its relationship with the natural world.

If nature is regarded primarily as an instrument for human advancement, even well-intentioned environmental measures may leave that underlying assumption untouched. The damage may be reduced without the relationship itself being reconsidered.

In Ecology, Community and Lifestyle, Næss explores the philosophical foundations of a different way of living. Ecology, community and personal conduct are not separate concerns. They belong to a broader question about the quality of our existence and the values by which we choose to organise it.

The enquiry is as intimate as it is ecological. It asks us to examine the habits of thought through which we distinguish what matters from what does not.

Beyond the Uses of Nature

Næss distinguished between what he called shallow ecology and deep ecology.

Shallow ecology concentrates primarily on environmental problems as they affect human beings. It seeks to reduce pollution, conserve resources and protect natural systems, often with the intention of safeguarding human health and prosperity.

Deep ecology proceeds from a more fundamental reconsideration. It recognises that living beings and natural systems possess value beyond their usefulness to us.

A woodland need not justify its existence through the timber it provides. A river need not be defended solely because it supplies water. The life of an animal is not made meaningful only by its relationship with human needs.

This does not require us to deny our own needs or pretend that human beings stand outside nature. Rather, it asks us to abandon the assumption that our interests are the only measure by which the living world should be understood.

There is a quiet dignity in recognising that other forms of life do not exist merely to complete our purposes.

A tree growing beside a path does not need to be admired to have value. Its branches may offer shelter; its roots may hold the soil; its leaves may respond to the changing light. Yet even these descriptions, useful as they are, do not exhaust the meaning of its existence.

To look at nature without immediately asking what it can provide is already a small change in consciousness.

The Ecological Self

One of Næss's most distinctive contributions is the idea of the ecological self.

We tend to imagine identity as something contained within the boundaries of the individual: our body, our possessions, our ambitions, our private history. The world beyond these boundaries becomes something external, something with which we must negotiate.

Næss proposed a more expansive understanding. As our sense of identity develops through meaningful relationships with other people, places and forms of life, our concern need not remain confined to the narrow interests of the isolated self.

A person who feels deeply connected to a particular landscape may come to protect it not merely because a rule demands it, or because its destruction would bring some immediate personal disadvantage. Care becomes an expression of that relationship.

The distinction is subtle but important. Responsibility imposed from outside can feel like a burden. Responsibility arising from a genuine sense of belonging may feel more like fidelity to something already recognised as part of one's life.

This is not a suggestion that human identity dissolves into nature, nor that every emotional attachment automatically produces sound ecological judgement. It is an invitation to reconsider where the boundaries of our concern might lie.

Perhaps we care for the world more readily when we cease to regard it as wholly separate from ourselves.

Community and the Good Life

The presence of the word community in Næss's title is significant. A meaningful ecological philosophy cannot be reduced to a collection of private gestures, however conscientious those gestures may be.

The way we live is shaped by the communities around us, by the expectations we inherit and by the systems that determine what is convenient, desirable or socially admired. An individual may wish to live more simply while inhabiting a culture that constantly encourages acquisition. A community may value its natural surroundings while making decisions that gradually diminish them.

The good life, therefore, requires more than individual restraint. It requires a broader conversation about what a community wishes to preserve and what it considers worth pursuing.

Næss questioned the assumption that prosperity and happiness necessarily increase with material consumption. A richer existence might instead include time for reflection, enduring friendships, creative work, direct contact with nature and the freedom to develop one's understanding of the world.

Such a life need not be austere. Beauty, comfort and pleasure have their place. The question is whether they enrich our experience or gradually become demands that govern it.

There is a difference between enjoying what we possess and organising our lives around the need to possess more.

The distinction becomes visible in ordinary circumstances: in the decision to walk rather than hurry, to repair rather than replace, to spend an afternoon outdoors rather than treat every unoccupied hour as an opportunity for consumption.

These acts are not a complete ecological philosophy in themselves. They may, however, express a different understanding of what makes life valuable.

The Mountain as a Place of Encounter

For Næss, the mountains were far more than a setting for leisure. His engagement with the natural world was inseparable from his philosophical interests, and his mountain retreat, Tvergastein, became closely associated with his life and thought.

There is something instructive about the mountain as a place of contemplation. Its scale unsettles our usual sense of proportion. Weather changes without consulting our plans. A path may become difficult beneath our feet. The landscape remains indifferent to our ambitions, yet offers a kind of companionship to those willing to encounter it attentively.

In such a place, thought need not be separated from experience. A philosophical question may accompany a climb; an idea may become clearer during a pause to observe the changing light upon the rocks.

The mountain does not provide answers in the manner of a book. It offers something less explicit and perhaps equally valuable: an opportunity to experience our own lives within a world that exceeds them.

We may return from such an encounter without having solved a single problem. Yet our sense of proportion may have changed.

The experience reminds us that we belong to a reality we did not create and cannot entirely control.

Rethinking Progress

Our age has become extraordinarily skilled at acceleration. Information travels rapidly, products appear and disappear, and the possibilities of technology continue to expand. Yet speed alone cannot tell us whether we are moving towards a better life.

Næss's philosophy invites us to question the equation of progress with ever-increasing power over the world. Greater capability does not automatically produce greater wisdom. The ability to transform a landscape does not establish that transforming it is desirable.

There are circumstances in which progress means developing something new. In others, it may mean learning to recognise what should not be lost.

A forest preserved from unnecessary destruction may represent a more thoughtful achievement than a new system designed to compensate for its disappearance. A community that protects the conditions of its own flourishing may demonstrate a deeper understanding of prosperity than one that measures success only through expansion.

These are not arguments against innovation. They are arguments for placing innovation within a wider understanding of value.

The question is not simply what humanity can accomplish, but what its accomplishments are intended to serve.

The Discipline of Wonder

Wonder is sometimes treated as a pleasant emotion, an agreeable response to a beautiful view or an unusual experience. In Næss's philosophy, the capacity to perceive the natural world differently carries a more serious implication.

What we notice influences what we value. What we value influences what we are willing to protect.

When a landscape is experienced merely as scenery, its destruction may appear to be a matter of competing interests. When it is understood as a living community with its own integrity, the same destruction becomes harder to regard as inconsequential.

Attention does not resolve every conflict between human needs and ecological responsibilities. Nor does appreciation alone replace scientific understanding, political action or practical conservation. But it can alter the assumptions from which those efforts begin.

A civilisation that rediscovers the ability to look carefully may also rediscover the reasons for exercising restraint.

The work begins quietly. We notice the weather before deciding whether it is convenient. We observe the life around us without immediately turning it into an object of use. We allow a place to matter without requiring it to provide an immediate reward.

In these moments, the natural world becomes less remote. We begin to understand that our relationship with it is not an optional interest added to an otherwise separate human life.

It is one of the conditions through which that life becomes intelligible.

The Art of Belonging

Arne Næss offers no simple escape from the contradictions of modern existence. His philosophy asks something more demanding than the adoption of a few improved habits. It asks us to reconsider the self, the community and the meaning of a good life.

The implications extend beyond environmental policy. They touch the way we inhabit our homes, arrange our priorities, understand prosperity and imagine our responsibilities to generations of living beings.

Perhaps belonging begins when we stop thinking of the Earth as a place that merely contains us.

A tree does not need to become a symbol before we can recognise its presence. A mountain does not need to be conquered before it can command our respect. The natural world does not need to justify itself through its service to humanity.

There is a form of wisdom in allowing things to possess a value beyond our immediate purposes.

To recover that wisdom is not to turn away from human life, but to understand it more fully. We are neither masters standing outside nature nor insignificant strangers within it. We are living beings among other living beings, dependent upon relationships whose complexity exceeds our understanding.

Ecology begins, then, not only with what we do, but with how we see.

And perhaps the first step towards protecting the world is to remember that we have never been separate from it.
