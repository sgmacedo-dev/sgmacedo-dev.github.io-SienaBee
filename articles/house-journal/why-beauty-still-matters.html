Why Beauty Still Matters

On beauty as a form of attention, a quiet discipline, and a way of remaining human.

By Silvana Macedo

26 October 2026

There are days when the world seems to have forgotten how to look.

Everything is presented to us at once: images, opinions, instructions, invitations to desire more and more quickly. We move through rooms without noticing the light upon the walls, pass trees without observing the shape of their branches, and listen to music while our minds are already elsewhere.

Beauty has not disappeared. Our attention has become difficult to hold.

Perhaps this is why beauty still matters.

Not because every room must be elegant, every object carefully chosen, or every afternoon arranged to resemble a painting. Beauty has little to do with the performance of a beautiful life. It begins somewhere quieter: in the willingness to notice what is already here.

A window opened after rain. The particular colour of an old book’s binding. A sentence that remains with us long after we have forgotten the circumstances in which we first encountered it. The hands of someone who has spent a lifetime making something well.

Such things ask nothing of us. They do not demand admiration. They simply become visible when we are sufficiently present.

The Difference Between Beauty and Display

We have grown accustomed to confusing beauty with its evidence.

A beautiful object is expected to announce its value. A beautiful home must be seen. A beautiful life must be documented, preferably in a manner that invites comparison. Even simplicity can become elaborate when it is arranged for an audience.

But beauty does not require witnesses.

A well-made chair may stand for generations in a room few people ever enter. A garden may be tended by someone who will never see it in full maturity. A letter may be written with extraordinary care and never published. Their value does not diminish because no one has been invited to applaud them.

There is a kind of beauty that becomes less itself when it is made to perform.

This does not mean that beauty should be hidden, or that sharing it is somehow vain. Art exists to be encountered; gardens are made to be walked through; music becomes itself in the act of being heard. The distinction lies elsewhere.

Are we attending to the thing itself, or to the impression it will produce?

The question is worth asking because the two desires can look remarkably similar. Both may lead us to choose carefully, cultivate taste, and seek excellence. Yet one is concerned with the quality of the encounter; the other, with the image of the person having it.

Beauty becomes impoverished when it serves only as proof of status. It becomes richer when it invites us beyond ourselves.

The Education of the Eye

Taste is often treated as an instinct, something one either possesses or lacks. But our capacity to recognise beauty can be cultivated.

We learn to see by looking again.

A painting that once seemed remote may gradually reveal its structure. A piece of music may disclose, on its tenth hearing, a tenderness that escaped us on the first. The architecture of an unfamiliar city becomes intelligible through its proportions, its materials, and the relationship between its public spaces and private lives.

Attention changes the object, or at least changes the world available to us through it.

This is one of the quiet gifts of literature. A writer gives us more than a sequence of events or a collection of sentences. At their best, writers refine our perception. They teach us to recognise motives, contradictions, gestures, and forms of suffering that we might otherwise have passed without understanding.

The same education can take place outside books.

A person who tends a garden learns the difference between growth and haste. Someone who restores furniture discovers that age is not always damage, and that repair can reveal a history that perfection conceals. A cook learns that generosity is sometimes expressed through the smallest decisions: the ripeness of a fruit, the balance of a meal, the attention given to the person who will receive it.

Beauty, in this sense, is not an ornament added to knowledge. It is one of the ways knowledge becomes intimate.

We begin by noticing a detail. We remain because the detail leads us towards a larger understanding.

The Moral Weight of Form

It may seem excessive to speak of beauty in moral terms. Surely goodness is a matter of conduct, not colour or proportion; surely justice is more urgent than the arrangement of a table.

Of course it is. No beautiful object can excuse cruelty, and no refined taste compensates for a failure of character.

Yet the way we make and maintain things is not entirely separate from the way we live among others.

Care is visible in form.

It appears in a public space designed to welcome rather than exclude, in an object made to be repaired rather than discarded, in a meal prepared with consideration for another person’s needs. It appears in language that has been revised until it says what it means without unnecessary injury or display.

These forms of care do not guarantee virtue. A polished surface can conceal an indifferent heart. But the deliberate practice of attention can remind us that other lives, other needs, and other forms of existence deserve consideration.

To make something well is to accept that the world will be affected, however slightly, by what we put into it.

The responsibility is modest, but real.

There is also a form of beauty that belongs to restraint. Not every space needs to be filled; not every argument needs its final word; not every possession needs to be replaced by a newer one. Sometimes the most intelligent decision is to leave room.

Restraint is not the rejection of abundance. It is the recognition that abundance becomes meaningful through selection.

A life can contain many beautiful things and remain inattentive. It can contain very few and be profoundly rich.

What Time Does to Beauty

We are often taught to admire what appears untouched by time.

Youth, novelty, flawless surfaces, the promise of beginning again: these have a particular power over the imagination. They suggest that life might be preserved from loss if only we chose correctly, cared sufficiently, or discovered the appropriate method.

But time has its own aesthetics.

Wood darkens. Linen softens. Books acquire the faint marks of repeated reading. A face becomes a record of expressions, griefs, affections, and years that cannot be recovered but need not be regretted.

Not everything that ages becomes beautiful. Some things break; some are neglected; some require repair. There is no wisdom in romanticising suffering or pretending that every loss is secretly a gift.

Still, the passage of time can give certain things a depth that novelty cannot provide.

We may come to love a room not because it has remained unchanged, but because it has accompanied us through changes. A familiar melody may become inseparable from a person we once knew. An old object may hold no monetary value and yet occupy a place in the life of a family that no replacement could fill.

Such beauty is inseparable from memory, but it is not merely sentimental. It reminds us that a life is not made meaningful by remaining untouched. Meaning accumulates through use, encounter, care, and the imperfect continuity of things.

Perhaps this is why the beautiful and the fragile so often appear together.

To notice beauty is also to recognise that it will not remain exactly as it is.

A More Attentive Life

There is no need to transform daily life into a project of aesthetic improvement.

The pursuit of beauty can become exhausting when every ordinary moment is expected to justify itself. A cup of tea need not be photographed. A walk need not produce an insight. A quiet room need not be arranged according to a doctrine of taste.

Life must be allowed to remain life: inconvenient, untidy, occasionally dull, and full of needs that cannot be answered by contemplation alone.

But within that life, we can recover small practices of attention.

We can read a page without immediately reaching for another source of information. We can listen to a piece of music without dividing our attention between several tasks. We can repair what remains useful, choose objects that deserve to accompany us, and allow an ordinary meal to be more than a hurried interruption.

We can learn to recognise beauty without needing to possess it.

A landscape does not become more beautiful because it belongs to us. A work of art does not become more profound because we can explain it. Another person’s dignity does not depend upon our ability to appreciate it.

Sometimes attention means staying. Sometimes it means leaving a thing undisturbed.

And sometimes it means admitting that we do not yet know how to see.

This, too, is an education: to approach the world without assuming that our first impression is its final meaning.

The Quiet Necessity of Beauty

Beauty cannot resolve every difficulty. It cannot replace justice, heal every wound, or relieve us of the practical responsibilities of living. There are circumstances in which survival must take precedence over contemplation, and in which the language of beauty can become a cruel luxury if it ignores the realities before us.

Its importance lies elsewhere.

Beauty reminds us that usefulness is not the only measure of value. That attention is more than a technique for becoming productive. That some experiences deserve to be received without being converted into an advantage, a possession, or a public statement.

It allows us to encounter the world as something more than material to be consumed or problems to be solved.

Perhaps, in the end, beauty matters because it asks us to participate in our own lives.

Not to observe them constantly from a distance, wondering how they appear, but to inhabit them: to notice the light, to hear the music, to recognise the person before us, to care for what has been entrusted to our keeping.

A beautiful life need not be exceptional.

It may be a life in which things are handled with care, words are chosen with thought, and ordinary hours are not always sacrificed to the next demand. A life in which the world is not expected to be beautiful at every moment, but is not passed over so quickly that its beauty becomes impossible to recognise.

The light will change. The music will end. The cup will cool beside the book.

Nothing needs to last forever to deserve our attention.

For now, it is enough to look.
